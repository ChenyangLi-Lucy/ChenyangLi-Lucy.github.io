Focusbot

A desk companion for programmers with ADHD, built to explore working alongside a robot.

Project Lead · Hardware, programming, user studies & paper writing

UbiComp Companion ’26

problem

Starting a task or returning after an interruption can be difficult for programmers with ADHD. In our needs study, reminders and productivity dashboards could feel like another demand on attention.

Working alongside someone can create a shared rhythm, but also brings scheduling and social pressure.

solution

Focusbot is a small robotic companion for the edge of the desk. Gentle arm movements, typing-like sounds and a slowly changing display make a work session feel shared.

A physical switch keeps the user in control. The prototype does not monitor code or record keystrokes.

Study 1 · Understanding focus challenges

53 survey responses and 15 interviews with programmers who reported ADHD traits informed the prototype.

Starting work

67.9% reported task-initiation difficulty. This led to invitation-style start cues.

Staying in the background

86.8% valued support that respected attention boundaries. Cues stay peripheral.

Feeling accompanied

Participants wanted acknowledgement without judgment. The robot creates a shared rhythm without recording productivity.

Focusbot probe

The probe combines typing-like motion and sound, an ambient time display, and a manual session switch. Original hardware and mechanical annotations are preserved.

Study 2 · Pilot deployment

Five participants with an ADHD diagnosis used the probe in their own workspaces: 10 minutes of setup, two hours of deep work, and a 30-minute interview.

Preliminary findings

Presence without judgment

Participants described a quiet peer and a feeling of being accompanied.

Time without countdown pressure

The gradual display change made session time noticeable in the background.

Shared momentum

Invitation-style prompts made starting feel like a shared activity.

Design considerations & next steps

Keep support peripheral, make shared rhythm legible, and give the user control over sessions. The five-person pilot offers early design insights; longer deployments are needed to study adoption and different sensory preferences.
