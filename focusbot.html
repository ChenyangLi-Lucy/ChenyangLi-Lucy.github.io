01 / The question

Can working alongside a robot make starting feel less solitary?

For the programmers in our needs study, the difficulty was often starting a task, recovering from interruptions, or staying with a frustrating problem. Another reminder or dashboard could become one more thing to manage.

Human body doubling—working alongside another person—can create a sense of shared presence. But it also requires scheduling and may bring the pressure of being observed. Focusbot asks what a quiet robotic companion could offer in that space.

02 / Interaction

Small cues at the edge of attention.

The prototype sits beside the laptop. Its movement, sound, and display make a work session feel shared while leaving the main task on the screen.

Synchronous Auditory Mirroring · SAM

A shared working rhythm

Soft typing-like audio and small servo-driven arm movements suggest a nearby co-worker. The rhythm is generated by the robot; it does not follow or record the user’s actual keystrokes.

Ambient Progress Visualization · APV

Time passing, gently

The display changes gradually through a work interval. This represents elapsed session time, without a numerical countdown or a productivity score.

Peer-framed transitions

An invitation to begin

Prompts such as “Let’s begin together” frame a transition as a shared start. A physical switch lets the user start or pause the session.

Sensing boundary: the pilot prototype has no camera, screen monitoring, or capture of code and keyboard input.

05 / Exploratory pilot

Taking the probe into real workspaces.

Five clinically diagnosed ADHD programmers took part in an exploratory pilot in their personal workspaces. Each session lasted approximately 160 minutes and involved coding or coding-adjacent writing.

10 minSetup & familiarization
120 minDeep-work session
30 minSemi-structured interview

Interview transcripts were examined using reflexive thematic analysis, with attention to perceived companionship, pressure, time awareness, and task transitions.

06 / What emerged

Accountability through co-presence.

“with me, not watching me”
P3 · Pilot interview

A quiet peer

Participants described a sense of company with less social evaluation. This points toward support that remains present without scoring or monitoring performance.

A “spatial” sense of time.
P2’s description of the gradual display change

Peripheral time awareness

The ambient cue made session time perceptible without repeatedly looking at a countdown. Participants found this less pressuring than a ticking timer.

“Let’s do it together” felt like we were a team.
Excerpt from P5’s pilot interview

A shared start

Peer-framed invitations were received more positively than imagined command-like prompts. Shared momentum emerged as a promising direction for task initiation.

What this study supports

These are preliminary qualitative observations from five short deployments. The pilot does not establish clinical effectiveness, productivity gains, or long-term adoption.

Next steps include multi-week use, comparison conditions, and personalization for sensory preferences. The design direction I want to carry forward is support that remains peripheral, makes its sensing boundaries clear, and gives the user control.
